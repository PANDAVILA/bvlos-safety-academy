To visualize how organizational flaws lead to operational accidents, aviation relies on Professor James Reason's Swiss Cheese Model of accident causation.

Imagine an organization's defenses against failure as a series of slices of Swiss cheese arranged one behind the other. Each slice represents a protective barrier: corporate policy, supervisory oversight, pre-flight checklists, and redundant aircraft systems. In an ideal world, these barriers are solid. In reality, every barrier has weaknesses, or holes, that are constantly opening, closing, and shifting position.

Accidents do not happen because of a single failure. They occur when the holes in every layer momentarily align, allowing a hazard trajectory to pass straight through all defenses.

Practitioners often describe four layers, following the HFACS framework that was built on Reason's work. Let's apply them to a realistic UAS scenario.

Layer 1, Organizational Influences. Management sets an aggressive daily quota for powerline inspections without allocating budget for spare batteries. That is a latent hole.

Layer 2, Unsafe Supervision. A Chief Pilot notices crews are rushing turnaround times between flights but fails to intervene. The second hole aligns.

Layer 3, Preconditions for Unsafe Acts. Ambient temperatures drop near freezing, increasing internal lithium-polymer battery resistance, while the GCS battery-health warning threshold is set too low. The third hole opens.

Layer 4, Unsafe Acts. Under pressure to finish the last corridor before sunset, the Remote Pilot commands an aggressive full-throttle climb. The battery experiences a sudden voltage sag, triggers a critical failsafe, and the aircraft descends uncontrolled onto a roadway.

Was the crash pilot error? Technically, the pilot moved the stick. Systemically, however, the accident was set up months earlier by organizational decisions. A robust SMS exists to detect and close those upstream holes before they align.
