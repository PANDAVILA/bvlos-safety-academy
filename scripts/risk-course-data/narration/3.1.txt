Now the sky. The air risk class, or ARC, is a qualitative class from a to d that describes how likely your aircraft is to meet a crewed aircraft. It depends on the airspace you fly in, not on your aircraft.

ARC-a means atypical or segregated airspace, with a very low encounter rate. ARC-b is a low encounter rate. ARC-c is medium. ARC-d is high.

Here is the simplified flow we use in this course. If the airspace is atypical or segregated, for example a restricted area where crewed traffic is excluded, the class is ARC-a. Above flight level six hundred, it is ARC-b. In an airport or heliport environment, it is ARC-d in class B, C or D airspace, and ARC-c otherwise. Above five hundred feet above ground level, a Mode C veil, a transponder mandatory zone or controlled airspace give ARC-d, and other airspace gives ARC-c. At or below five hundred feet, a Mode C veil, controlled airspace or an urban area give ARC-c, and uncontrolled rural airspace gives ARC-b.

In a real dossier, follow the figure and the airspace encounter categories of the SORA version your authority accepts, and justify your choice with airspace data.

Some common mistakes. Using the lowest altitude of the route instead of the highest point of the volume. Forgetting helicopters, aerial work, gliders and paragliders at low level. Treating class G as empty, because crewed aircraft fly there too, often without a transponder. And forgetting the contingency volume when you check the airspace.

An example. A rural pipeline inspection flown at one hundred and twenty feet above ground, in uncontrolled airspace away from airports, is ARC-b. The same route flown at six hundred feet becomes ARC-c. Altitude alone changed the class.
