## What you will learn

- How final GRC and residual ARC combine into the SAIL
- What the SAIL drives in the rest of the dossier
- How to test whether a design change is worth its effect on SAIL

## The SAIL table

| Final GRC | ARC-a | ARC-b | ARC-c | ARC-d |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 or 2 | I | II | IV | VI |
| 3 | II | II | IV | VI |
| 4 | III | III | IV | VI |
| 5 | IV | IV | IV | VI |
| 6 | V | V | V | VI |
| 7 | VI | VI | VI | VI |
| above 7 | certified category | | | |

SAIL I and II are low assurance, III and IV medium, V and VI high. The SAIL does not say how risky the operation is. It says **how much proof** you must provide.

## What SAIL drives

- The **robustness** you need for each Operational Safety Objective (OSO)
- The **containment** requirement
- How much third-party validation and testing the authority expects

## Worked examples

1. Pipeline inspection: final GRC 2 and residual ARC-b gives **SAIL II**.
2. Same UA at 600 ft AGL, residual ARC-c, final GRC 2 gives **SAIL IV**. Staying below 500 ft AGL saves a lot of evidence.
3. Urban delivery: final GRC 4, residual ARC-c gives **SAIL IV**.
4. Final GRC 6 and ARC-d gives **SAIL VI**, the highest assurance level.

## Use the table as a design tool

Before you invest in an extra mitigation, ask what it does to the SAIL. If a parachute lowers the final GRC from 4 to 3 but the ARC stays c, the SAIL stays IV and the evidence burden does not fall. Spend the effort where the table moves.

<div class="callout"><strong>Tip:</strong> keep a one-page "what if" sheet. Showing the assessor that you compared options and picked the cheapest one that meets the risk is good practice.</div>
